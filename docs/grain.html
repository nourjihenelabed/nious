One order, nine steps, one table

The engine computes every waterfall number once, at the transaction-line grain, and derives every aggregate from that one table by groupby. This page walks a single real order — TRX-00011 — through all nine stages so you can watch the data change, makes the case for the grain decision, and opens with a defect the walkthrough turned up.

Open first · unresolved · affects every published figure

One deduction is being subtracted twice

The waterfall takes list_value − gross_value as a “negotiated off-list” step, then separately subtracts the on-invoice discount edges. On this snapshot those are the same money. For 515 of the 530 certified transactions the two agree to floating-point precision — not approximately, exactly — which means the line’s unit_price is already net of the on-invoice discount, and the engine deducts it a second time.

Two independent checks in the raw data agree: gross_amount equals the sum of line totals for every transaction, and net_amount equals gross_amount minus the off-invoice edges for every transaction. So the snapshot’s own fields already describe the waterfall as list → on-invoice → invoice (= gross_amount) → off-invoice → pocket (= net_amount), with no separate negotiated step at all. The net_amount field the Week 2 write-up flagged as “reversed convention” turns out to be exactly the pocket price.

This is a question for the data owner, not a patch to apply quietly. The engine’s formula is correct for data where line prices are gross of discount; it is wrong for data where they are net, and this snapshot is the second kind. Ask what unit_price on a transaction line means. If it is net — which the evidence says — then negotiated_off_list should be dropped and invoice_price should be gross_value, and Week 2’s open question 4 has its answer: the 11.9% “negotiated gap” is not a real waterfall step.

Everything else on this page survives it. The grain decision is about where facts are stored; this is about what one step means. Stage 5 below lets you switch between the two readings and watch the consequences, which is a better thing to bring to a review than either number alone.

The case, and the objections it has to survive

The claim is narrow, so state it narrowly: store the facts once, at the finest grain at which any measure is observed; make every aggregate a view of that table, never a second calculation. Here are the five questions most likely to come back at you, and what the run output actually says.

Line grain is false precision — the discounts aren’t line-level.

Half-true, and worth conceding immediately. List value, gross value and COGS are observed at line grain — quantity × a product attribute, or the line total itself. On-invoice discount and off-invoice leakage are not; they hang off the transaction and are allocated pro-rata by line_total.

The table stores both kinds and marks which is which, and the assumption sits behind one named constant, ALLOCATION_METHOD. It is lossless: the parts sum back to the transaction to the cent under any method, so every aggregate at transaction grain and above is untouched by the choice. Change the method in stage 4 and watch the transaction total refuse to move. Below transaction grain the choice genuinely matters — which is exactly why it is one named constant rather than an expression buried in a join.

Why not just build the aggregates you actually need?

Six aggregations ship today — segment, customer, product, channel, region, period. Each is a groupby over the same table with zero additional arithmetic. The moment a waterfall is computed twice, the two copies drift, and the drift surfaces as a number a client disputes in a meeting.

It is also the only design in which reconciliation is a test rather than a hope: every aggregation must sum back to the grand total, step by step, on every run. This run: 54 of 54 checks pass, largest disagreement 3.7 × 10⁻⁹. That check would be meaningless against an independently computed aggregate — it would only be comparing two guesses.

What does the fine grain actually buy you? Show me.

This order. At transaction grain TRX-00011 is a single healthy number: 14.6% pocket margin. At line grain the same order is −0.5%, 34.1% and 30.7% — the margin is carried entirely by two lines while the largest line by value, AC Drives A100, sits fractionally below cost. That line is invisible one level up, not because it is hidden but because it has already been averaged away.

It generalises. Across the 336 multi-line certified deals, the median gap between the best and worst line inside a single deal is 13.9 margin points, and the top decile exceeds 24. A per-deal margin number is an average over that spread every time.

State the caveat before you are asked: under the restated waterfall only 3 lines book-wide are below cost, 2 of them inside otherwise profitable deals. The dispersion finding holds under both readings; the below-cost count does not. Quote the dispersion.

Mixing measures of different grains in one fact table is a modelling error.

Correct as a general rule, and the objection to take most seriously. The textbook alternative is two fact tables — fact_line for the line-observed measures, fact_transaction_discount at its own natural grain — combined at query time by drill-across on the conformed transaction key.

One table was chosen because the deliverable is the nine-step waterfall per line. Splitting the storage does not remove the allocation; it moves it into every consumer, so Track B’s narrative layer, the notebooks and the dashboard each re-implement it — which reintroduces exactly the drift the single table exists to prevent.

The concession worth offering: the split remains available and does not disturb the grain decision. The atomic line table is what would feed both halves anyway, so this is a storage-layout question deferred, not a design foreclosed.

What happens to rows with missing data?

Missing is never zero. A step sums to UNAVAILABLE if any contributing row lacks it, and certification cascades from line to transaction — a transaction is certified only when every one of its lines is complete, so a half-known deal is excluded whole rather than reported half.

170 of 700 transactions fall out, and each is named: 116 lines missing unit_cost, 60 missing list_price, 12 with a discount of unknown amount, and 210 lines dragged out by an incomplete sibling line. Coverage — 75.7% — is reported beside every published number, with the denominator taken from the full population rather than the certified subset. Take it from the subset and every group reports 100% coverage by construction, which is how a dashboard ends up lying without anyone lying.

Would you ship this?

Separate the two questions, because they have different answers. The grain decision is the durable part and should not change. The implementation around it is a single-machine, full-recompute demo, and most of it would be replaced. Three rows below are not migration items but correctness gaps that exist today.

ConcernTodayIn productionVerdict
Grain Atomic — one row per transaction line. Unchanged. Atomic grain is what lets a question nobody anticipated still be answerable. It is the cheapest decision to get right early and the most expensive to reverse. keep
Step definition negotiated_off_list and on_invoice_discount are the same money, deducted twice. Resolve before anything is published. Confirm with the data owner what unit_price on a line means; if net of on-invoice discount, drop the negotiated step and set invoice_price = gross_value. Add a run-time assertion that the two are not equal, so the next dataset cannot reintroduce it silently. fix
Point-in-time correctness list_price and unit_cost are read as the product’s current values and joined onto historic lines. Re-running March’s waterfall next quarter prices it with next quarter’s list, so history changes quietly underneath a published number. Fix by freezing list_price and unit_cost onto the fact at transaction time, or by Type-2 effective-dating on the product dimension with an as-of join. The first is cheaper and enough for now. fix
Currency Five currencies, no FX rate; cross-currency groups abstain. Blocked on a business answer, not an engineering one. Either amounts are already normalised and the abstention relaxes, or the graph needs a dated FX table — in which case rate-as-of-transaction-date is the same point-in-time problem as the row above, and should be solved the same way. ask
Compute pandas, in-process, whole snapshot in memory. 1,408 lines. The arithmetic is joins, column arithmetic and one groupby-transform — it translates to SQL almost line for line. Push the fact build into the warehouse (dbt model) or DuckDB/Polars past roughly 10⁶–10⁷ lines. Nothing about the model changes. replace
Refresh Every run recomputes the entire book. Partition by period and materialise incrementally. The subtlety: an off-invoice rebate edge can land months after its transaction, so late-arriving discounts must trigger a recompute of the affected partition — otherwise incremental silently freezes stale leakage. replace
Serving One CSV per aggregation, rewritten each run. Pre-materialised aggregate tables for dashboard latency — which contradicts none of this. They become caches over the atomic table rather than parallel sources of truth, and the reconciliation check is precisely what proves a cache is still honest. replace
Table width 45 columns, dimensions denormalised onto every line. Fine at snapshot scale and genuinely convenient. At volume, either narrow the fact to keys plus measures and join dimensions at query time, or keep it wide as a deliberate choice on columnar storage, where repeated low-cardinality strings compress to almost nothing. either
Allocation Pro-rata by line_total, one constant. Unchanged until the graph exposes a line-level DISCOUNTED_BY edge. If it does, one constant changes and every downstream number follows — which is the whole return on having isolated it. keep

If you get sixty seconds

The version to say out loud. Lead with the defect — bringing your own bug to a review is the strongest position in the room, and it makes the grain argument arrive on its own.

“Talk me through the engine.”

“Before the numbers — I found a step that’s counted twice. The line price is already net of the on-invoice discount, so subtracting the discount edges again double-deducts it. It matches exactly on 515 of 530 certified transactions, and the snapshot’s own net_amount field confirms the intended waterfall. It moves book margin from 18% to 30%. I want to confirm what unit_price means before I publish either number.”

“And why is the fact table at line grain?”

“Because deal-level margin is an average that hides its own spread. Order TRX-00011 reads 14.6%; inside it the three lines are −0.5%, 34% and 31%, and the biggest line is below cost. Across 336 multi-line deals the median spread between best and worst line is about 14 margin points.”

“So it’s computed once, at line grain, and all six aggregations are groupbys over that one table — no second calculation to drift. Every aggregation is checked back against the grand total on every run; 54 of 54 pass.”

“The one assumption is that transaction-level discounts are allocated to lines pro-rata by line value. It’s exact at transaction grain and above, it’s an assumption only at product grain, and it’s one named constant to change if the graph ever gives us a line-level discount edge.”

“What I’d fix before production is point-in-time: list price and unit cost are joined as current values, so re-running an old period silently reprices it.”
